Topic 1

Crystal Field Theory — Foundations

Failures of Werner's Theory

Werner's theory was groundbreaking but left several questions unanswered:

  • Could not explain why complexes adopt different geometries (octahedral, square planar, tetrahedral, etc.)
  • Could not explain the color and magnetic properties of complexes
  • Did not explain why some complexes are more stable than others
  • Did not describe the nature of the metal–ligand bond

Valence Bond Theory (VBT) — Pauling

Linus Pauling proposed VBT to explain structure, hybridization, and high/low spin complexes.

Key Postulates

  • Metal center must have vacant s, p, d, f orbitals
  • These hybridize to form degenerate orbitals for ligand donation
  • Ligands donate ≥2 electrons (coordinate covalent bond)
  • Strong-field (SF) ligands force spin pairing; weak-field (WF) ligands do not
  • (n−1)d involved → inner orbital / low-spin complex
  • nd involved → outer orbital / high-spin complex

Hybridization → Geometry

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → sp³d² (outer orbital, high-spin)  |  [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ → d²sp³ (inner orbital, low-spin)
[Ni(Cl)₄]²⁻ → sp³ (tetrahedral, high-spin)  |  [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ → dsp² (square planar, low-spin)

VBT Failures

VBT could not explain: colors of complexes · why some ligands are SF vs WF · optical properties · reaction rates · thermodynamic stability of complexes

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) — Bethe & van Vleck (1930)

CFT treats the metal–ligand interaction as purely electrostatic. It was originally developed for crystalline ionic solids, then applied to coordination complexes.

Key Assumptions

  • Only metal d-orbitals participate — no orbital overlap
  • Interaction is purely ionic/electrostatic
  • Anionic ligands → point charges; neutral ligands → dipoles
  • d-orbitals of a free metal ion are degenerate
  • Complex stability arises from electrostatic attraction (lowers overall energy)

d-Orbital Shapes (assumed)

  • dx²−y², dz² — lobes along axes (eg set)
  • dxy, dxz, dyz — lobes between axes (t2g set)
  • dz² is unique: axially symmetric from Y₂⁰(θ,φ) ∝ (3cos²θ − 1); has dumbbell + torus shape
dz² full name: d2z²−x²−y²

Crystal Field Splitting — Octahedral Field (Oh)

In an octahedral ligand field, 6 ligands approach along ±x, ±y, ±z axes. d-orbitals split into two sets:

  • Free metal in vacuum → all 5 d-orbitals degenerate
  • Spherical electrostatic field → all raised equally in energy
  • Octahedral field breaks degeneracy: orbitals along axes (dx²−y², dz²) face ligands directly → raised
  • Orbitals between axes (dxy, dxz, dyz) → lowered
  • Oh is centrosymmetric → labels carry "g" subscript
t₂g (dxy, dxz, dyz): −2/5 Δₒ each  ·  eᵍ (dx²−y², dz²): +3/5 Δₒ each
Net energy change = 0 (baricentre rule preserved)

Crystal Field Splitting — Tetrahedral Field (Td)

In a tetrahedral field, 4 ligands approach along cube body diagonals — none point directly at any d-orbital. The splitting is inverted vs octahedral and smaller:

  • dxy, dxz, dyz (t₂ set) → closer to ligand approach → raised
  • dz², dx²−y² (e set) → further from ligands → lowered
  • Td is NOT centrosymmetric → no "g" subscript on labels
t₂: +2/5 Δt  ·  e: −3/5 Δt
Δt ≈ 4/9 Δo (same metal, same ligand)

Measuring Δ — UV-Visible Spectroscopy

Electronic transitions between split d-orbitals absorb visible light. From the absorption peak wavelength λmax:

E = hc/λ  →  Δ (in cm⁻¹) = 1/λ(cm)

Example: [TiIII(H₂O)₆]³⁺ — λmax = 493 nm → Δₒ = 20,300 cm⁻¹ = 243 kJ/mol
Ground state: t₂g¹ eᵍ⁰  → hν →  Excited state: t₂g⁰ eᵍ¹

Spectrochemical Series & Limitations of CFT

Ligands ranked by ability to split d-orbitals (weak → strong field):

I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ < N₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < NH₃ < en < bpy~phen < NO₂⁻ < PR₃ < CN⁻ ~ CO
◄ Weak field (π-donor)        Strong field (π-acceptor) ►

CFT contradiction: halide ordering

CFT predicts F⁻ (smallest, closest approach) should give the largest Δ. But in reality: I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻. CFT cannot explain this — orbital interactions (covalency) are needed.

Ligand bonding types

  • σ-only donors: NH₃, H₂O — middle of series
  • σ + π donors (filled p → t₂g): Cl⁻, Br⁻, OH⁻ — reduce Δ (weak field)
  • σ donor + π acceptor (empty π* ← t₂g back-bond): CO, CN⁻, PPh₃ — increase Δ (strong field)

These covalency effects are addressed by Ligand Field Theory (LFT), which extends CFT by incorporating orbital overlap and partial covalent character.

Pairing Energy (P) and Spin State

Whether electrons pair in the lower t₂g orbitals or spread into higher eg depends on comparing Δ vs P (pairing energy). P depends only on the metal, not the ligands.

Two components of P = Pₑ + Pᴄ

  • Coulombic repulsion (Pₑ): two electrons in the same orbital repel — major contribution
  • Exchange energy loss (Pᴄ): electrons in different orbitals with parallel spins gain exchange stabilization; pairing loses this
Δ > P → electrons pair in t₂g → low-spin
Δ < P → electrons spread to eᵍ → high-spin

Only d⁴–d⁷ configurations can be either high- or low-spin in an Oh field.

Spin Crossover (SCO)

When Δₒ ≈ P, external stimuli can switch a complex between spin states.

Mechanisms

  • Thermal SCO: low T → low-spin (lower enthalpy); high T → high-spin (higher entropy, more microstates). Transition at T½
  • Pressure-induced SCO: high pressure shortens M–L bonds, increases Δ → favours low-spin
  • LIESST (Light-Induced Excited Spin-State Trapping): light excites complex to a metastable spin state; basis for molecular switches and optical memory
Example: [Fe(phen)₂(NCS)₂] (d⁶ Fe²⁺)

Low T → low-spin: t₂g⁶ (S = 0, diamagnetic)
Heat/light → high-spin: t₂g⁴ eᵍ² (S = 2, paramagnetic)

Accompanied by: bond lengthening (Fe–N) · color change · magnetic change

Key Observations

  • 4d and 5d metals generally have larger Δo than 3d metals → almost always low-spin
  • Tetrahedral Δt ≈ 4/9 Δo → rarely large enough to force pairing → low-spin Td complexes are uncommon
  • High-spin / low-spin ambiguity exists only for d⁴–d⁷ octahedral 3d metal complexes
Topic 2

Crystal Field Stabilization Energy (CFSE)

What is CFSE?

CFSE is the extra stability a transition metal complex gains when its d-orbitals split in a ligand field. It equals the energy difference between the ligand field arrangement and the hypothetical isotropic (unsplit) field.

CFSE = ΔE = Eligand field − Eisotropic field

Key factors: Geometry (Oh/Td/SP) · Number of d-electrons · Spin pairing energy (P) · Ligand field strength

Octahedral (Oh) Rules

eg
+3/5 Δo per electron — destabilizing
t2g
−2/5 Δo per electron — stabilizing

Add pairing energy P for each forced pair beyond the isotropic baseline.

Worked Example — d⁷ Octahedral

High-spin t₂g⁵ eg²

CFSE(HS) = 5×(−2/5) + 2×(+3/5) = −4/5 Δo

Low-spin t₂g⁶ eg¹

CFSE(LS) = 6×(−2/5) + 1×(+3/5) + P = −9/5 Δo + P

Jahn-Teller Theorem & Distortions

The Theorem (1937)

A non-linear molecule with a degenerate electronic ground state will distort to remove that degeneracy and lower its energy.

Degeneracy = Instability → distortion removes it → stability gained
📄
Sheldon Cooper
"Degeneracy in quantum systems is like having two people equally qualified for the roommate spot — the system must break the tie. I broke mine with a 31-page Roommate Agreement."

Elongation (Z-out) — most common

Axial ligands move away → dz² drops, dx²−y² rises. Relieves repulsion from four equatorial ligands pointing at dx²−y².

Compression (Z-in) — rare

Axial ligands move closer → dz² rises, dx²−y² drops.

Topic 6

Spinels and Inverse Spinels

General Formula: AB₂X₄

A = divalent cation (2+), B = trivalent cation (3+), X = anion (usually O²⁻). The oxide sublattice forms a cubic close-packed (FCC) arrangement of O²⁻ ions. This creates two types of interstitial voids: 8 tetrahedral (Td) sites and 4 octahedral (Oh) sites per formula unit — but only some are occupied.

Catalysts Lithium-ion batteries Ferromagnets Pigments & dyes Ceramic magnets Biomedical imaging (MRI)

The Crystal Structure in Detail

In the spinel unit cell (space group Fd3̄m), the oxide ions occupy the 32e positions of an FCC lattice. This generates:

  • 64 Td holes total — only 8 are occupied per unit cell (⅛ occupancy)
  • 32 Oh holes total — only 16 are occupied per unit cell (½ occupancy)
  • Ratio of Oh : Td occupied = 2 : 1 — this is why B appears twice in AB₂O₄
Unit cell: 8 × (AB₂O₄) = 8A in Td + 16B in Oh + 32O²⁻ (normal spinel)

Why Do Ions Choose Their Sites? — The CFSE Argument

The key driver of normal vs. inverse structure is the Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE) gained by each ion in each site type. Recall:

CFSE (Oh) is always larger than CFSE (Td) by a factor of ≈ 4/9 for the same Δ

This means ions that gain large CFSE in an Oh field are strongly driven into Oh sites, sometimes forcing another ion into the "wrong" Td site — creating an inverse spinel.

Normal Spinel

(A²⁺)tet [B³⁺₂]oct O₄

A²⁺ occupies Td sites. B³⁺ fills all Oh sites.

Occurs when A²⁺ has no strong CFSE preference, or when B³⁺ has a larger CFSE in Oh (e.g., Cr³⁺, Co³⁺ LS).

Examples: MnAl₂O₄, ZnFe₂O₄, FeCr₂O₄, Co₃O₄

Inverse Spinel

(B³⁺)tet [A²⁺ B³⁺]oct O₄

Half the B³⁺ is pushed into Td. A²⁺ and the remaining B³⁺ share Oh.

Occurs when A²⁺ has a strong preference for Oh, evicting B³⁺ into Td (B³⁺ is indifferent, like Fe³⁺ d⁵ HS).

Examples: Fe₃O₄, NiFe₂O₄, CoFe₂O₄, MgFe₂O₄

The Inversion Parameter (λ)

Real spinels often lie between fully normal and fully inverse. The inversion parameter λ (0 ≤ λ ≤ 1) quantifies this:

General: (A²⁺₁₋λ B³⁺λ)tet [A²⁺λ B³⁺₂₋λ]oct O₄
λ = 0 → Normal spinel  |  λ = 1 → Fully Inverse spinel

At high temperatures, cation disorder increases (λ → intermediate), because entropy favours random distribution. Quenching from high T can freeze in a disordered structure.

Step-by-Step: How to Determine Normal or Inverse

  1. Identify the two cations and assign their d-electron counts.
  2. Determine the spin state (high-spin in an oxide lattice unless strong-field ligand).
  3. Look up or calculate CFSE for each ion in both Oh and Td sites.
  4. The ion with the larger CFSE difference (Oh − Td) wins the Oh site.
  5. If B³⁺ is d⁵ high-spin (CFSE = 0 everywhere), A²⁺ always wins Oh → inverse spinel.
  6. If B³⁺ is low-spin or a d³ ion with very large Oh preference, B³⁺ wins Oh → normal spinel.
Δ(CFSE) = CFSE(Oh) − CFSE(Td) → ion with largest Δ goes into Oh

Magnetic Consequences of Spinel Structure

The site occupancy directly controls the magnetic behaviour of spinel oxides via superexchange interactions through the bridging O²⁻ ions:

  • A–O–B superexchange (Td–O–Oh): spins on A and B tend to align antiparallel (antiferromagnetically). If A and B have different numbers of unpaired electrons, the moments don't fully cancel → ferrimagnetism.
  • Fe₃O₄: Fe³⁺ in Td (5 unpaired, up) is antiparallel to Fe³⁺ in Oh (5 unpaired, down) → cancel. Fe²⁺ in Oh (4 unpaired, up) is left uncompensated → net moment of 4 µB per formula unit → ferrimagnetic.
  • ZnFe₂O₄ (normal): Zn²⁺ has no unpaired electrons in Td → the Fe³⁺–O–Fe³⁺ Oh–Oh superexchange dominates → antiferromagnetic, no net moment.
  • NiFe₂O₄ (inverse): Fe³⁺ Td and Fe³⁺ Oh cancel; Ni²⁺ Oh (2 unpaired) gives net moment of ~2 µB → ferrimagnetic, used in soft magnets.
Ferrimagnetism = antiparallel spin alignment with unequal moments → net ≠ 0

Topic 9

Ferromagnetic & Antiferromagnetic Coupling

Ferromagnetism

Spins align parallel. Net magnetic moment is strong. Exchange interaction favors parallel spins.

Examples: Fe metal, Mn-acetate (SMM)

Antiferromagnetism

Spins align antiparallel. Net moment is very low or zero. Superexchange via bridging ligands (O²⁻, Cl⁻).

Examples: Ni(II) acetate dimers

Selection Rules

Laporte Rule (centrosymmetric)

Only g ↔ u transitions are allowed. Δl = ±1 required.

s↔p, p↔d, d↔f → ALLOWED
s↔s, p↔p, d↔d → FORBIDDEN

d–d transitions in Oh are Laporte-forbidden → weak (ε ≈ 1–100)

Spin Selection Rule

Total spin must not change during transition.

ΔS = 0 → ALLOWED
ΔS ≠ 0 → FORBIDDEN

[Mn(H₂O)₆]²⁺: all d-d spin-forbidden → pale pink
